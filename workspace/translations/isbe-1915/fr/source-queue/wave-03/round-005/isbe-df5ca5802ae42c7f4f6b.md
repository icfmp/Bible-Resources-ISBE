# COLOR; COLORS
<!-- ISBE-END:1 -->

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:2 -->
kul'-er, kul'-erz: The word translated "color" in the King James Version is `ayin, which literally means "eye" or "appearance," and has been so translated in the Revised Version (British and American). In the New Testament the Greek prophasis, has the meaning of pretense or show (⟦ISBE:KEEP:3:Ac 27:30⟧; compare ⟦ISBE:KEEP:4:Re 17:4⟧ the King James Version). The references to Joseph's coat of many colors (⟦ISBE:KEEP:5:Ge 37:3⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:5:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:6:13⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:6:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:7:12⟧) and "garments of divers colors" (⟦ISBE:KEEP:8:2Sa 13:18⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:8:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:9:19⟧) probably do not mean the color of the garment at all, but the form, as suggested in the American Revised Version, margin, "a long garment with sleeves." In ⟦ISBE:KEEP:10:Jud 5:30⟧ the word for "dip" or "dye" appears in the original and has been so translated in the American Standard Revised Version. (see DYE). In ⟦ISBE:KEEP:11:1Ch 29:2⟧ riqmah, meaning "variegated," hence, "varicolored," is found. In ⟦ISBE:KEEP:12:Isa 54:11⟧, pukh is used. This name was applied to the sulfide of antimony (Arabic kochl) used for painting the eyes. Hence, the American Revised Version, margin rendering "antimony" instead of "fair colors" (see ⟦ISBE:OPEN:13⟧PAINT⟦ISBE:CLOSE:13⟧). In ⟦ISBE:KEEP:14:Eze 16:16⟧ Tala', is found, meaning "covered with pieces" or "spotted," hence, by implication "divers colors."

Although the ancient Hebrews had no specific words for "color," "paint" or "painter," still, as we know, they constantly met with displays of the art of coloring among the Babylonians (⟦ISBE:KEEP:16:Eze 23:14⟧) and Egyptians and the inhabitants of Palestine Pottery, glazed bricks, glassware, tomb walls, sarcophagi, wood and fabrics were submitted to the skill of the colorist. This skill probably consisted in bringing out striking effects by the use of a few primary colors, rather than in any attempt at the blending of shades which characterizes modern coloring. That the gaudy show of their heathen neighbors attracted the children of Israel is shown by such passages as ⟦ISBE:KEEP:17:Jud 8:27⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:17:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:18:Eze 23:12⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:18:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:19:16⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:19:tail⟧

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:20 -->
Two reasons may be given for the indefiniteness of many of the Biblical references to color.
<!-- ISBE-END:20 -->

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:21 -->
(1) The origin of the Hebrew people: They had been wandering tribes or slaves with no occasion to develop a color language.
<!-- ISBE-END:21 -->

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:22 -->
(2) Their religious laws: These forbade expression in color or form (⟦ISBE:KEEP:23:Ex 20:4⟧). Yielding to the attractions of gorgeous display was discouraged by such prophets as Ezekiel, who had sickened of the abominations of the Chaldeans (⟦ISBE:KEEP:24:Eze 23:14⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:24:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:25:15⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:25:tail⟧⟦ISBE:KEEP:26:16⟧); "And I said unto them, Cast ye away every man the abominations of his eyes" (⟦ISBE:KEEP:27:Eze 20:7⟧⟦ISBE:FIX:27:tail⟧
<!-- ISBE-END:22 -->

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:28 -->
Indefiniteness of color language is common to oriental literature, ancient and modern. This does not indicate a want of appreciation of color but a failure to analyze and define color effects. The inhabitants of Syria and Palestine today delight in brilliant colors. Bright yellow, crimson, magenta and green are used for adornment with no evident sense of fitness, according to the foreigners' eyes, other than their correspondence with the glaring brightness of the eastern skies. A soapmaker once told the writer that in order to make his wares attractive to the Arabs he colored them a brilliant crimson or yellow. A peasant chooses without hesitation a flaring magenta or yellow or green zun-nar (girdle), rather than one of somber hues. The oriental student in the chemical or physical laboratory often finds his inability to distinguish or classify color a real obstacle. His closest definition of a color is usually "lightish" or "darkish." This is not due to color blindness but to a lack of education, and extends to lines other than color distinctions. The colloquial language of Palestine today is poor in words denoting color, and an attempt to secure from a native a satisfactory description of some simple color scheme is usually disappointing. The harmonious color effects which have come to us from the Orient have been, in the past, more the result of accident (see ⟦ISBE:OPEN:29⟧DYE⟦ISBE:CLOSE:29⟧) than of deliberate purpose, as witness the clashing of colors where modern artificial dyes have been introduced.
<!-- ISBE-END:28 -->

<!-- ISBE-BLOCK:30 -->
This inability of the peoples of Bible lands to define colors is an inheritance from past ages, a consideration which helps us to appreciate the vagueness of many of the Biblical references.

(14) White:
